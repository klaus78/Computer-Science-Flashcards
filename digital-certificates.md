## Digital Certificates

<details>
<summary>What is a digital certificate?</summary>
A digital certificate is a kind of electronic passport. A digital certificate is used to guarantee that a digital message comes from a reliable and verified source and without data losses.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Why a digital certificate is also knows as X.509?</summary>
A digital certificate is also known as X.509 because it is built according to the ITU-T recommendation X.509,
the first formal international standard that defined how digital certificates and public key infrastructures (PKIs) are to be structured.

The ITU-T (International Telecommunication Union – Telecommunication Standardization Sector), an agency of the United Nations, publishes technical telecommunication and networking standards called "Recommendations."

The ITU-T groups its standards into lettered series based on subject matter:

* V-series: Data communication over telephone networks (e.g., V.90 modems).

* X-series: Public data networks, systems interconnection, and internetworking security.

Within the X-series, standards in the X.500 through X.599 range govern Directory Services—the conceptual predecessors to global address books and modern directories like LDAP and Active Directory.

Recommendation X.509 was drafted throughout the mid-1980s and officially published in 1988 (formally approved on November 25, 1988) by the CCITT (now the ITU-T), in collaboration with ISO/IEC.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the core technology behind digital certificates?</summary>
The core technology behind digital certificates is the Public Key Infrastructure (PKI).

Public key infrastructure is based on asymmetric cryptography and cryptographic hashing.
</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the anatomy of a digital certificate?</summary>
A **certificate** is divided into three main parts:

- **TBSCertificate ("To Be Signed")**: it contains the core of the certificate, including identity, keys, constraints, and validity
- **SignatureAlgorithm**: Specifies the cryptographic algorithm used by the CA to sign the certificate (e.g., `sha256WithRSAEncryption` or `ecdsa-with-SHA256`).
- **SignatureValue**: the actual cryptographic signature generated by the CA using its private key over the TBSCertificate block.
</details>

<details>
<summary>What is contained  in the "TBSCertificate" block of a digital certificate?</summary>
The **TBSCertificate** block contains information about who the certificate is for, who issued it, and how it can be used.
The detailed information is:

* **Version**: Specifies the X.509 version number (v1, v2, or v3). Modern certificates are almost universally v3, which supports extensions.

* **Serial Number**: A unique positive integer assigned by the CA to every certificate it issues. Combined with the issuer's name, this uniquely identifies the certificate and is crucial for tracking revocations via CRLs (Certificate Revocation Lists) or OCSP.

* **Issuer**: The distinguished name (DN) of the Certificate Authority that verified the identity and signed the certificate (e.g., `CN=Let's Encrypt Authority X3, O=Let's Encrypt, C=US`).

* **Validity Period**: is the window of time the certificate is trusted, consisting of two timestamps:

  * Not Before: The start date/time.
  * Not After: The expiration date/time.

* **Subject**: The entity (individual, organization, server, or device) to whom the certificate is issued (e.g., CN=`example.com`).

* **Subject Public Key Info**: Contains two vital pieces of information:
  * The Public Key Algorithm identifier (e.g., RSA or ECDSA).
  * The actual Public Key bytes belonging to the subject.
</details>

<details>
<summary>What is a certificate authority (CA)? What does its main tasks?</summary>
A **certificate authority (CA)** is a trusted organization or entity that issues certificates. 

The main tasks of a certificate authority are:

* **Identity Verification**: Before issuing a certificate, the CA checks whether the requester actually owns the domain name or represents the organization they claim to be.

* **Certificate Issuance**: Once verification succeeds, the CA binds the verified identity to the public key by creating an X.509 certificate and signing it with its own CA Private Key.

* **Revocation Management**: If a private key is stolen, a server is compromised, or a certificate expires early, the CA publishes revocation data so browsers know to stop trusting that certificate immediately (via CRLs or OCSP).
</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the difference between public and private CAs?</summary>
The difference in short between public and private CAs:

* **Public CAs** are trusted globally by all browsers and devices, used to secure public websites.

* **Private CAs** are trusted internally only by a specific organization, used to secure private networks, internal servers, and corporate devices.
</details>

<details>
<summary>How Do Your Devices Trust a CA?</summary>

Devices trust a CA because operating systems and web browsers come pre-installed with a built-in list of approved Root CAs (known as a Trust Store).

When a device evaluates a certificate, it checks if the certificate chains back to one of these pre-approved root authorities. If the chain leads to a root stored in the device's trusted list, the device implicitly trusts the connection.
</details>



<details>
<summary>What are the steps to check the validity of a certificate?</summary>
To check if a digital certificate is valid, a browser or client performs these steps in order:

1. **Check Expiry Dates**: verify that the current date and time fall between the certificate's "Not Before" and "Not After" timestamps.

2. **Verify the Signature**: Use the issuer's public key to decrypt the digital certificate's signature and match it against the hash of the certificate data to confirm it hasn't been tampered with.

3. **Trace the Trust Path**: Follow the chain of certificates upward from the leaf to an Intermediate CA and ultimately to a Root CA pre-installed in the device's Trust Store.

4. **Check Revocation Status**: Query the Certificate Authority via OCSP (Online Certificate Status Protocol) or check a CRL (Certificate Revocation List) to ensure the certificate hasn't been prematurely revoked due to a compromised key.

5. **Verify Hostname**: Confirm that the domain name in the URL matches the Subject Alternative Name (SAN) listed inside the certificate.
</details>

<details>
<summary>What is a certificate lifecycle?</summary>

The **certificate lifecycle** refers to the stages a digital certificate goes through from creation to retirement:

1. **Generation & Request**: Generating a cryptographic key pair and sending a Certificate Signing Request (CSR) to a CA.

2. **Validation**: The CA verifies the requester's domain ownership or legal identity.

3. **Issuance**: The CA signs the certificate and issues it to the requester.

4. **Deployment**: Installing the certificate on a server or device to secure traffic.

5. **Renewal or Revocation**: Automatically renewing the certificate before its expiration date, or revoking it early if the private key is compromised.
</details>
